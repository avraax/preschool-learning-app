# Motivation science, gamification and dark patterns for children aged 4-7

Scope note: ~20 searches/fetches. Primary sources were not always reachable (PMC returned a reCAPTCHA page; Michigan Medicine returned 403), so several findings rest on publisher abstracts, university pages or reputable secondary summaries — flagged where it matters. Items marked "[not re-verified this session]" are well-known citations with stable DOIs that I did not re-fetch; the writer should treat them as lower confidence.

## 1. Overjustification and extrinsic rewards in young children

### Takeaway
Expected, tangible rewards made contingent on doing an activity reduce young children's later free-choice interest in it; unexpected rewards and informational verbal feedback do not, and positive verbal feedback tends to help. The effect is strongest exactly in the population and reward type a sticker economy resembles: children, tangible, expected, engagement/completion-contingent.

### Cited Findings
- Lepper, Greene & Nisbett (1973), JPSP 28(1):129-137: preschoolers who already liked drawing with "magic markers" were split into expected-award (certificate promised in advance), unexpected-award, and no-award groups. Only the expected-award group later spent significantly less free time with the markers; the unexpected-award group did not differ from no-award. The effect was demonstrated in children who already liked the activity. Sample size is reported inconsistently by secondary sources (51 vs 69) — check original. — [SFU Educational Review summary](https://journals.lib.sfu.ca/index.php/sfuer/article/download/371/242/1037); [Greene, Sternberg & Lepper 1976 follow-up PDF](https://eclass.uowm.gr/modules/document/file.php/NURED262/%CE%A0%CE%A1%CE%A9%CE%A4%CE%9F%CE%A4%CE%A5%CE%A0%CE%91%20%CE%91%CE%A1%CE%98%CE%A1%CE%91/Greene%20et%20al%20overjustification%201976.pdf); DOI 10.1037/h0035519
- Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999), Psychological Bulletin, meta-analysis of 128 experiments: engagement-contingent, completion-contingent and performance-contingent tangible rewards all significantly undermined free-choice intrinsic motivation (d = -0.40, -0.36, -0.28 respectively). Engagement- and completion-contingent rewards also reduced self-reported interest (d = -0.15, -0.17). — [Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999 PDF (UW)](https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf)
- Same meta-analysis: positive verbal feedback *enhanced* free-choice behaviour (d = 0.33) and self-reported interest (d = 0.31). — [Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999](https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf)
- Same meta-analysis: tangible rewards were *more* detrimental for children than for college students, and verbal rewards were *less* enhancing for children than for college students. — [Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999](https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf); [SDT org 2001 follow-up in Review of Educational Research](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2001_DeciKoestnerRyan.pdf)
- Unexpected tangible rewards did not undermine motivation in the meta-analysis (consistent with Lepper 1973). — [Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999](https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf) [the specific "unexpected = no effect" figure was not quoted in the retrieved snippet; it is a well-known result of this paper — verify in full text]
- Contested: Cameron & Pierce (1994) concluded the undermining effect was minimal; Deci et al. argued that analysis was seriously flawed; Cameron, Banko & Pierce (2001) replied that negative effects occur only under limited conditions. — [Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999](https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf); [Ewha repository record of the 2001 reply exchange](https://pure.ewha.ac.kr/en/publications/extrinsic-rewards-and-intrinsic-motivation-in-education-reconside/)
- Warneken & Tomasello (2008), Developmental Psychology 44(6):1785-1788: 20-month-olds who received a material reward for helping were later *less* likely to help than those who got social praise or nothing — an overjustification effect at the earliest age tested. Authors conclude early helping is intrinsically motivated and extrinsic-reward socialisation can undermine it. — [Duke Scholars record](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1458228); [Hepach et al. 2012 (same lab) describing the finding](https://sites.duke.edu/tomasellolabduke/files/2016/09/Hepach_etal_2012.pdf); DOI 10.1037/a0013860

### Inferences
- An XP ring that fills from every task and pays a sticker is structurally an *expected, engagement/completion-contingent, (virtually) tangible* reward — the category with the largest undermining effect sizes. The mitigations the literature supports: keep the sticker low-salience relative to the activity, make it feel like a surprise/celebration rather than a promised wage, never verbally frame tasks as "do this to get a sticker", and pair it with informational verbal feedback (the one reward form that *raises* interest).
- The undermining effect is measured on *free-choice* return to the activity when rewards are withdrawn. In an app where the reward never stops, the practical risk is that children engage with the ring rather than the content (interest transfers to the reward), and that interest in the underlying skill (letters, numbers) outside the app is not built.
- Whether a *digital* sticker counts as "tangible" is not established in this literature — see gaps.

### Gaps
- No study found that tests digital stickers/virtual collectibles specifically against the overjustification paradigm in 4-7-year-olds.
- I could not retrieve Deci et al.'s child-only subgroup effect sizes or the unexpected-reward effect size verbatim.

## 2. Self-Determination Theory, the "Four Pillars", and chocolate-covered broccoli

### Takeaway
Learning-science consensus for young children's apps is that the learning has to be the game: "minds-on" active, engaged-not-distracted, meaningful, and socially interactive (Hirsh-Pasek et al. 2015). SDT adds autonomy, competence and relatedness as the needs whose satisfaction sustains intrinsic motivation; gamification layered on top ("chocolate-covered broccoli") is the anti-pattern.

### Cited Findings
- Hirsh-Pasek, Zosh, Golinkoff, Gray, Robb & Kaufman (2015), "Putting Education in 'Educational' Apps: Lessons From the Science of Learning", Psychological Science in the Public Interest 16(1):3-34. Four pillars: active ("minds-on", not merely tapping/swiping), engaged (with the learning material, not distracted by extraneous features), meaningful (connects to the child's life/prior knowledge), socially interactive. Plus a fifth consideration: a clear learning goal. — [APS publication page](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/educational-apps.html); [Penn State record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/putting-education-in-educational-apps-lessons-from-the-science-of/); DOI 10.1177/1529100615569721
- The paper's "active" pillar explicitly excludes physical tapping/swiping; a swipe-to-steer spaceship game is their example of an app lacking cognitive demand. They propose evaluating apps on a grid of "Pillar Score" × "Educational Context" (how high the learning goals are). They cite ~80,000 apps labelled educational in Apple's App Store as of Jan 2015, largely untested. — [New America summary](https://newamerica.org/education-policy/edcentral/new-framework-identify-truly-educational-apps); [Bold Science summary](https://boldscience.org/what-makes-an-app-educational/)
- Bold Science's summary adds that the app should let children proceed at their own pace (control). — [Bold Science](https://boldscience.org/what-makes-an-app-educational/)
- SDT's three needs (autonomy, competence, relatedness) appear directly in the UNICEF/LEGO RITEC-8 wellbeing framework for digital play: safety & security, diversity/equity/inclusion, autonomy, competence, emotions, relationships, creativity, identities. Developed with children mostly aged 8-12 across 18 countries; safety/security is mandatory, DEI fundamental, others combinable. — [UNICEF RITEC page](https://www.unicef.org/innovation/responsible-innovation-technology-children-ritec); [UNICEF: Designing digital play with children's well-being in mind](https://www.unicef.org/designing-digital-play-childrens-well-being-mind); [NYU CREATE RITEC project](https://steinhardt.nyu.edu/create/research/lego-ritec-project)
- "Chocolate-covered broccoli" critique: Habgood & Ainsworth (2011), "Motivating children to learn effectively: Exploring the value of intrinsic integration in educational games", J. Learning Sciences 20(2):169-206 — children (7-11) learned more, and chose to play longer, when the maths was integrated into the core game mechanic than when the same game content was used as a reward wrapper around quiz questions. [not re-verified this session] — DOI 10.1080/10508406.2010.508029

### Inferences
- An endless quiz with an XP ring and sticker ceremony is partly an "extrinsic wrapper" design; its ethical/learning strength depends on the quiz itself being minds-on and on the reward layer staying subordinate. The design's existing choices (no stars, scores, bests, time pressure) remove most of the performance-contingent, ego-involving elements that SDT predicts are controlling.
- The pillar with least obvious coverage in a solo tablet quiz is "socially interactive" — see §6 (joint media engagement).
- RITEC was developed with 8-12-year-olds; applying it to 5-year-olds is extrapolation.

### Gaps
- No primary empirical test of SDT need satisfaction specifically in 4-7-year-olds' app play was found.

## 3. Specific mechanics: gamification evidence, collectibles, companions/teachable agents, praise, feedback, streaks

### Takeaway
Gamification meta-analyses show small-to-moderate positive learning effects, but mostly from older learners and with unstable motivational effects. For young children, the best-supported mechanic-level evidence concerns *praise wording* (process/action praise beats person/trait praise, especially after failure) and *companion characters you teach* (protégé effect, though tested in older children). Evidence on collectibles, customisation, choice and streaks specifically at ages 4-7 is thin.

### Cited Findings
- Sailer & Homner (2020), Educational Psychology Review 32(1):77-112, meta-analysis: gamification has small positive effects on cognitive (g = .49), motivational (g = .36) and behavioural (g = .25) learning outcomes; only the cognitive effect was stable in methodologically rigorous studies. Competition combined with collaboration looked effective for motivation. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w); [Augsburg OPUS PDF](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/files/109056/109056.pdf)
- Li, Ma & Shi (2023), Frontiers in Psychology: g = 0.822 across 41 studies — estimates vary widely by inclusion criteria. — [PMC10591086](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10591086/)
- Children-specific meta-analyses are small: Fadhli et al. (2020), ages 6-10, only 6 studies, ES 1.01 — too few studies to generalise. A 2025 early-childhood meta-analysis (Lathifah et al.) reports positive effects "particularly when game elements are tailored to developmental characteristics". — [UMPO eprint](https://eprints.umpo.ac.id/5837/); [SciTePress 2025](https://scitepress.org/PublishedPapers/2025/140690)
- Mueller & Dweck (1998), JPSP 75(1):33-52, six studies with fifth graders: praise for intelligence (vs effort) led to more performance-goal focus, and after failure less persistence, less enjoyment, more low-ability attributions and worse performance; intelligence-praised children chose easier problems. — [Columbia PDF](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/psychology/courses/3615/Readings/Mueller_Dweck.pdf); DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.75.1.33
- Cimpian, Arce, Markman & Dweck (2007), Psychological Science 18:314-316, with 4-year-olds: generic praise ("you are a good drawer") vs non-generic ("you did a good job drawing") made no difference after success, but after mistakes the generic-praise children denigrated their skill, felt worse, avoided fixing mistakes and quit more. Directly relevant age band. — [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-praise-children); [ScienceBlogs summary](https://scienceblogs.com/cognitivedaily/2007/09/13/in-1999-melissa-kamins-and) (secondary; original not fetched); DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01896.x
- Chase, Chin, Oppezzo & Schwartz (2009), J. Science Education & Technology 18(4):334-352, "Teachable Agents and the Protégé Effect": students put more effort into learning for a teachable agent (Betty's Brain) than for themselves; benefits strongest for lower achievers. Fifth graders attributed mental states and responsibility to their agent and were more willing to acknowledge errors (proposed mechanisms: responsibility, revision-based improvement, "ego protection" — failure belongs partly to the agent). Tested in 5th and 8th grade, not preschool. — [Stanford PURL](https://purl.stanford.edu/zm369yx3532); DOI 10.1007/s10956-009-9180-4
- Radesky's team found characters in preschool apps used to pressure children (e.g. characters urging the child to act when idle, characters pushing purchases) — the dark side of companion characters ("parasocial relationship pressure"). — [Deceptive Patterns summary of Radesky 2022](https://deceptive.design/articles/prevalence-and-characteristics-of-manipulative-design-in-mobile-applications-used-by-children); [Meyer et al. 2019, Michigan Medicine](https://michiganmedicine.org/health-lab/advertising-kids-apps-more-prevalent-parents-may-realize)

### Inferences
- Narration copy should praise the action/process ("Du fandt B'et!", "Du prøvede igen") rather than the child's trait ("Du er så klog"); the Cimpian effect appears at age 4 and only shows up after errors — i.e. exactly when an endless quiz will deliver them.
- A mascot can legitimately carry the "relatedness" need and possibly a light protégé framing ("help the mascot find…"), which may also soften error feedback (ego protection). The line it must not cross is *pressure*: a mascot that is sad when the child leaves, begs them to keep playing, or nags when idle is the documented manipulative pattern.
- Streaks: no direct child study found, but streaks are a loss-aversion mechanic tied to returning daily; they combine engagement-contingent reward with time pressure and guilt — the patterns regulators and Radesky's taxonomy flag. The app's "no bests/no streaks" stance is consistent with the evidence.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed 4-7-year-old study found on: virtual collectibles/sticker books, customisation, narrative framing, choice, feedback timing, or streaks. Habgood & Ainsworth is the nearest on narrative/game integration (ages 7-11).
- No study found that tests the protégé effect at ages 4-7.
- No good source found on optimal error-handling design (e.g. retry vs show answer) for this age.

## 4. Dark patterns in children's apps and the regulatory lines

### Takeaway
Manipulative design is near-universal in apps young children use (~80% of apps; ads in 95%), concentrated in free apps and lower-SES households. Documented patterns to avoid: parasocial pressure from characters, fabricated time pressure, navigation constraints (can't exit/ads blocking), lures/rewards for longer play or purchases, and ad pressure. Regulators (UK ICO Children's Code standard 13; Apple Kids Category) prohibit nudges that exploit psychological bias and commercial/data flows without parental gates.

### Cited Findings
- Radesky, Hiniker, McLaren, Akgun, Schaller, Weeks, Campbell & Gearhardt (2022), "Prevalence and Characteristics of Manipulative Design in Mobile Applications Used by Children", JAMA Network Open 5(6):e2217641: apps used by 160 children aged 3-5; only ~20% of apps had no manipulative design; ~99% of children had at least one such design in a top-used app. — [Deceptive Patterns summary](https://deceptive.design/articles/prevalence-and-characteristics-of-manipulative-design-in-mobile-applications-used-by-children); [PMC9206186](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9206186/) (not fetchable this session); DOI 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2022.17641
- Categories named: parasocial relationship pressure, fabricated time pressure, navigation constraints, attractive lures (to prolong play or buy), advertisement-based pressure. Aims: prolonging gameplay, prompting re-engagement, purchase pressure, forced ad viewing. — [Deceptive Patterns](https://deceptive.design/articles/prevalence-and-characteristics-of-manipulative-design-in-mobile-applications-used-by-children); [Newswise/Michigan press release](https://www.newswise.com/articles/study-design-tricks-commonly-used-to-monetize-young-children-s-app-use)
- Secondary sources report manipulative features in 95% of free vs 80% of paid apps; more common in apps used by children of parents without a college degree. — [The Digital Parent (secondary)](https://thedigitalparent.substack.com/p/designed-to-deceive); [Deceptive Patterns](https://deceptive.design/articles/prevalence-and-characteristics-of-manipulative-design-in-mobile-applications-used-by-children). Note: Radesky's 2024 Vermont testimony cites "80% of 133 apps", conflicting with the 160-child framing (probably 160 children vs 133 distinct apps — unverified). — [Vermont testimony PDF](https://legislature.vermont.gov/Documents/2024/WorkGroups/Senate%20Economic%20Development/Bills/S.289/Witness%20Documents/S.289~Jenny%20Radesky~Kids%20Code%20Testimony~3-1-2024.pdf)
- Young children struggle to distinguish ads from gameplay; Radesky argues still-developing executive function (self-control, decision-making) makes children more susceptible to persuasive design. — [DTNext report](https://www.dtnext.in/lifestyle/wellbeing/design-tricks-are-often-used-to-monetize-childrens-app-usage-study); [Senate Commerce testimony](https://www.commerce.senate.gov/services/files/4D82755F-D188-46F8-B063-DAC676ADCC5E)
- Meyer, Adkins, Yuan, Weeks, Chang & Radesky (2019), J. Dev. & Behav. Pediatrics: 135 apps (96 most-downloaded "5 and Under" Google Play apps after a 39-app pilot); 95% had at least one form of advertising, 100% of free apps; pop-up video ads, commercial characters pushing in-app purchases, banner ads in 17% (27% of free). — [Michigan Medicine](https://michiganmedicine.org/health-lab/advertising-kids-apps-more-prevalent-parents-may-realize); [UBC-hosted PDF](https://blogs.ubc.ca/etec523/files/2021/01/00004703-201901000-00004.pdf)
- UK ICO Age Appropriate Design Code, standard 13 "Nudge techniques": do not use nudges to lead children to provide unnecessary personal data or weaken privacy protections; exploiting psychological bias conflicts with GDPR fairness/transparency and Recital 38. (Some summaries say it also covers nudges to extend use; the ICO page retrieved did not include that wording.) — [ICO standard 13](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/guide-to-data-protection/ico-codes-of-practice/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/13-nudge-techniques); [Code PDF v2.1](https://ico.org.uk/media/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services-2-1.pdf)
- Apple Kids Category (guideline 1.3): no third-party ads/analytics in the strict 2019 version, later relaxed to allow limited analytics without identifiers and human-reviewed contextual ads; parental gate required before commercial activity or outbound links; no IDFA/PII to third parties. Current text not retrieved — check developer.apple.com. — [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/?p=1880829); [Apple Developer Forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/117320); [GoodBarber help](https://www.goodbarber.com/help/apple-rejection-r100/apple-rejection-apps-for-kids-a365/)

### Inferences
- Bright lines for this app, mapped to the taxonomy: the mascot must never guilt, beg, or react sadly to leaving (parasocial pressure); no countdowns or "only today" (fabricated time pressure); exit/back must always be available, the ceremony must not trap the child (navigation constraint); the sticker ceremony must not be dangled as a reason to continue ("one more task to your sticker!") — that is an attractive lure to prolong play. The XP ring being visible near completion is itself a mild lure (goal-gradient); keeping it quiet rather than narrated is the defensible version.
- Being free with no ads puts the app outside the free-app/low-SES pattern the research most criticises — a real ethical differentiator.

### Gaps
- Per-category prevalence numbers from Radesky 2022 not retrieved (PMC blocked).
- No EU DSA (Art. 28 / 2025 guidelines on minors), Danish Medierådet/Forbrugerombudsmanden guidance, FTC, or 5Rights "Disrupted Childhood" content retrieved in this session — needs a follow-up search.
- No source found specifically on streaks or loss-aversion mechanics in children's apps.

## 5. Attention span, session length, and seductive details / decoration

### Takeaway
Extraneous visual decoration measurably reduces kindergarteners' attention and learning (Fisher et al. 2014), and "seductive details" reduce learning across studies (g ≈ -0.33), more so for novices and when learners can't control pacing. I found no robust empirical norm for app session length at ages 5-7.

### Cited Findings
- Fisher, Godwin & Seltman (2014), Psychological Science 25:1362-1370: 24 kindergarteners, six science lessons in a lab classroom; with heavily decorated walls children were more distracted, spent more time off task, and showed smaller learning gains than with bare walls. Fisher did not recommend stripping classrooms, rather checking whether displays distract. — [PubMed 24855019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24855019/); [CMU news](https://www.cmu.edu/news/stories/archives/2014/may/may27_decoratedclassrooms.html); DOI 10.1177/0956797614533801
- Commentary: Imuta & Scarf (2014) argue novelty rather than irrelevance may drive the distraction, and that decoration relevance matters. — [PMC4274873](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4274873/)
- Sundararajan & Adesope (2020), "Keep it coherent", Educational Psychology Review 32:707-734: 68 effects / 58 studies, g = -0.33 for seductive details; worse for novices and when learners cannot control pacing; sources disagree on other moderators. Typical studies were 6-12-minute sessions, cognitive outcomes only. Rey (2012) found similar medium negative effects. — [ACU Inspire summary](https://inspire.acu.edu.au/articles/seductive-details); [WSU news](https://news.wsu.edu/2020/03/19/seductive-details-inhibit-learning/); [QIC-WD umbrella summary](https://qic-wd.org/node/583)
- Hirsh-Pasek et al. (2015) "engaged" pillar: features that distract from the learning goal (extraneous animations, sounds) undermine learning. — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/educational-apps.html)

### Inferences
- The parallax world, ambient mascot motion and idle animations are, in Fisher's terms, the "decorated walls". Evidence supports keeping them visually quieter during the task than between tasks, and avoiding novelty spikes (new decoration) during question answering. Novelty (Imuta & Scarf) suggests a stable, familiar world is less distracting than a changing one.
- Seductive-details evidence is mostly older learners and text; extrapolating to 5-year-olds likely understates harm (novices, weaker executive function).

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed session-length norm for 5-7-year-olds in learning apps found; popular "attention span = 2-3 min per year of age" rules have no solid source and should not be cited.
- No study found on decoration/animation density within tablet apps for this age.

## 6. Joint media engagement and social rewards

### Takeaway
Adult-child co-use is associated with small but positive gains in children's learning from digital media (0-6 years); app features shape how much parent-child talk happens. Rewards that are social (praise, showing a parent) did not undermine helping in toddlers, unlike material rewards.

### Cited Findings
- Taylor et al. (2024) meta-analysis: 17 studies, 100 effect sizes, N = 1,288 children aged 0-6, 1977-2022: small positive association between adult-child co-use and learning from digital media. — [UCL Discovery PDF](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10199543/1/Taylor%20et%20al%202024.pdf)
- Mathers et al. multilevel meta-analysis: 15 experimental studies, 627 parent-child dyads, 190 effects, children 2-7: app/e-book design features shape adult-child interaction, especially oral language. — [ORA Oxford](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:f0e456c2-c34f-4f9e-92b2-726ccfaa7e9a/files/rg732db659)
- Koch, Barr & Sundqvist (2024) Joint Media Engagement Scale (ages 1-5) separates co-viewing from co-playing games. — [PMC11823321](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11823321/) [attribution from search snippet; verify]
- Warneken & Tomasello (2008): social praise did not reduce later helping, material reward did. — [Duke Scholars](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1458228)
- Deci et al. (1999): positive verbal feedback raises interest (d ≈ 0.31-0.33). — [UW PDF](https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf)

### Inferences
- A sticker book the child *shows to a parent* converts a tangible-ish reward into a social, conversational one — the form least likely to undermine motivation and the one that adds the missing "socially interactive" pillar. Designing the book as a conversation piece (what is this? where did you get it?) is better supported than making it a private collection.
- Since the app runs on the child's own iPad without a parent present, JME is opportunistic; small prompts that invite a parent are evidence-aligned, but none should pressure the child.

### Gaps
- No study found specifically on "show a parent" rewards in apps for this age.
- Moderators of the Taylor et al. co-use effect (content type, age) not retrieved.
