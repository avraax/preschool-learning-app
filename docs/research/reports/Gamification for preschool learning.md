# Make the learning act the game

Børnelæring should stop looking for engagement in reward layers and put it into the game action itself: the evidence and the best comparable apps agree that children aged 5-7 learn more, and play longer, when the thing they manipulate *is* the letter or the number, while expected rewards wrapped around a quiz are the one mechanic with a measured cost to intrinsic motivation. The app has already removed nearly every reward wrapper the research warns against (stars, bests, rounds, streaks, shops, timers), so its remaining weakness sits in the core loop: 9 of its 16 task games share one verb, "hear or see a prompt, tap one of N tiles", and the owner's own children stopped playing at v1.0, when that loop was dressed in far more extrinsic reward than it is today. The free tier of recommendations (0-500 DKK) is therefore mostly design work Claude Code can do: replace flat tap-quizzes with manipulation verbs, rewrite feedback as informational process language, let a correct answer cause something in the board, tie the sticker book to curriculum, and run a handful of home playtests. The expensive tier (up to tens of thousands of DKK) buys craft, not mechanics: a Rive-animated mascot, a commissioned art set for game boards, and a Danish voice actor, the last of which would require the owner to relax the "Azure TTS only" rule. The single game to act on is a **remake of Tal Quiz** into a harbour game, provisionally *Fyld Båden*, where the child hears an inverted Danish number word ("syvogtredive") and loads the boat with pallets of ten and single crates, so that decoding the word and building its quantity are one act. It reuses Tal Quiz's route, gameId, difficulty ranges, generator, practice ledger and all 100 prebaked number clips, and it can ship at roughly zero cash cost.

## Comparable apps split into reward economies and toys

The leading early-learning apps fall into two families. Curriculum apps wrap a lesson path in a meta-economy: Khan Academy Kids lets children "collect bugs, hats, and toys for their favorite characters" with a cast led by Kodi the bear, who also "celebrates with them, jumping with excitement" on success ([Khan Academy blog](https://blog.khanacademy.org/?p=74), [Khan Kids help centre](https://khankids.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/360049358751-Learn-more-about-the-characters-inside-Khan-Academy-Kids)); Reading Eggs pays **Golden Eggs into an "Eggy Bank"** spent on avatar clothes and house furnishings, plus a critter per lesson kept in a Critter Book ([Reading Eggs KB](https://kb.readingeggs.com/celebrate-learning/3-rewards-systems)); ABCmouse runs tickets and a reward store that parents say "pull attention away from learning" ([Common Sense Media](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/website-reviews/abcmousecom)); Lingokids awards stickers and caps its free tier at **three activities a day** as a conversion lever ([Pastory](https://pastory.app/articles/is-lingokids-good-and-safe-for-kids/)). Toy-like apps put the whole reward inside the loop instead. Endless Alphabet has "no high scores, failures, limits or stress" and teaches through "talking letters and a short animation illustrating the definition" ([Originator](https://www.originatorkids.com/endless-alphabet/)); in Busy Shapes the successful placement *is* the reward, garnished with a small penguin animation ([Common Sense Media](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/app-reviews/busy-shapes-colors)).

The most instructive group is the Nordic and European game-first titles, because they turn the curriculum unit into an object. DragonBox Numbers makes numbers into **Nooms that can be "stacked, sliced, combined, sorted, compared"** ([Games for Change](https://www.gamesforchange.org/games/dragonbox-numbers/)); Fiete Math has children **load ships with boxes that group and separate like building blocks**, with no time limits ([Fatherly](https://www.fatherly.com/gear/fiete-apps-by-ahoiii-are-educational-open-ended-games)); Poio's Readlings eat letters, and **mastered words are written into a fairy-tale the child can read aloud** — a collection that is itself learning ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/-/id1529174573)). Replay in these apps comes from a world the play builds (Moose Math's town, Poio's houses, Fiete's harbours) or from a toy with no end state (Pok Pok, whose founders say "if a child gets stuck, we've made a mistake") ([Common Sense Media](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/app-reviews/moose-math-by-duck-duck-moose), [BetaKit](https://betakit.com/?p=332907)), not from scores.

Danish children see little of this. Poio ships in Norwegian and Swedish but **not Danish**, GraphoGame has a Norwegian version but no Danish one surfaced, and the Danish letter apps teachers list — Læserejsen, Bogstavernes lyde, Hit med lyden — are mostly hear-a-sound, tap-the-letter drills, the same shape Børnelæring already has ([App Store: Poio](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/-/id1529174573), [Dansk Indskoling (CFU)](https://cfuindskolingdansk.wordpress.com/apps/)). The most praised Danish title, Knæk Læsekoden, is singled out for teaching **letter sounds rather than letter names** ([Børne App Guiden](https://www.boerneappguiden.dk/knaek-laesekoden/)). A Danish game where the letter or number is something the child acts on is an open niche.

None of these apps proves a mechanic causes learning. Outcome studies exist for Khan Kids (a vendor-linked RCT, 49 low-income 4-5-year-olds, ~13 minutes a day for 10 weeks, effect size **0.72** on pre-literacy) and Homer (a vendor-hosted NYU study whose "74%" is a phonological-awareness gain only), but neither isolates a gamification feature ([Khan Academy blog](https://blog.khanacademy.org/khan-academy-kids-improves-pre-literacy-skills-in-preschoolers-research-confirms/), [Homer study PDF](https://site-static.learnwithhomer.com/static/pdf/can-an-app-close-the-literacy-gap-efficacy-learn-with-homer-susan-neuman-nyu.pdf)). DragonBox School showed early-numeracy and motivation gains in a ~1,400-pupil quasi-experiment ([AERA](https://www.aera.net/Publications/Online-Paper-Repository/AERA-Online-Paper-Repository-Viewer/ID/1888824)), and GraphoGame's 19-study meta-analysis ranges from **−1.07 to +1.58**, with orthographic consistency a suspected moderator — a warning for Danish, one of the least consistent orthographies ([Ahmed et al. 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2020.00132/text)). The only feature-level engagement numbers come from Duolingo's adult app, and they are small: streak and milestone animations raised day-7 retention by **+1.7%** ([Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit)).

## Expected rewards cost interest; feedback and integration build it

The best-established finding in this field cuts directly against reward economies. In Lepper, Greene and Nisbett's 1973 study, preschoolers promised a certificate for drawing later chose to draw less, while children given the same certificate unexpectedly did not ([SFU Educational Review](https://journals.lib.sfu.ca/index.php/sfuer/article/download/371/242/1037)). Deci, Koestner and Ryan's meta-analysis of 128 experiments found engagement-, completion- and performance-contingent tangible rewards all reduced free-choice motivation (**d = −0.40, −0.36, −0.28**), found the damage **larger for children than for students**, and found that positive verbal feedback *raised* free-choice behaviour (**d = +0.33**) ([Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999](https://depts.washington.edu/techdocs/papers/deciExtrinsicRewardsAndIntrinsicMotivation99.pdf)). The effect reaches toddlers: 20-month-olds given a material reward for helping later helped less, while those given praise did not ([Duke Scholars](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1458228)). The result is contested at the edges (Cameron and Pierce argued the effect is limited), and no study tests digital stickers specifically, but the direction is consistent.

Børnelæring's XP ring and sticker ceremony is structurally an expected, completion-contingent reward: every task adds 5-6 XP, and a sticker arrives every ~8 tasks in chapter 1 and every ~20-24 thereafter ([progression.ts](../../../src/config/progression.ts), [rewards-and-progression.md](../../../.claude/rules/rewards-and-progression.md)). That does not make it wrong — the app has already stripped the ceremony to one quiet picture, removed the narrated countdown, and keeps the path deterministic — but it means the reward layer is the place the evidence says to *hold steady*, not to grow. The literature's mitigations are already partly in place: keep the sticker low-salience, never frame a task as "do this for a sticker", and pair it with informational verbal feedback.

The positive evidence points to integration. Hirsh-Pasek and colleagues' four pillars require learning to be **active ("minds-on", which explicitly excludes mere tapping), engaged rather than distracted, meaningful and socially interactive** ([APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/educational-apps.html)). Habgood and Ainsworth found 7-11-year-olds learned more and chose to play longer when the maths was the core mechanic rather than a quiz wrapped in game rewards ([DOI 10.1080/10508406.2010.508029](https://doi.org/10.1080/10508406.2010.508029); not re-verified this session). Gamification meta-analyses show small positive effects (g = .49 cognitive, .36 motivational), with **only the cognitive effect stable** in rigorous studies ([Sailer & Homner 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w)) — which is to say gamification helps when it makes children practise more of the right thing, not when it makes them feel rewarded.

Two mechanic-level results transfer cleanly to this age. Praise wording matters: Cimpian and colleagues found that **4-year-olds** given generic praise ("you are a good drawer") felt worse, avoided fixing mistakes and quit more *after an error* than those praised for the act ([BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-praise-children)), and Mueller and Dweck showed intelligence praise made fifth graders pick easier problems ([Columbia PDF](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/psychology/courses/3615/Readings/Mueller_Dweck.pdf)). An endless quiz delivers errors constantly, so its narration should name what the child did. And decoration has a cost: kindergarteners in a heavily decorated classroom spent more time off task and learned less ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24855019/)), and seductive details reduce learning at **g ≈ −0.33**, worse for novices ([ACU Inspire](https://inspire.acu.edu.au/articles/seductive-details)). Spectacle belongs after the answer, not during the task — which the app's frozen, dimmed in-game world already respects ([scene-and-world.md](../../../.claude/rules/scene-and-world.md)).

The harms are well documented. Radesky's JAMA study of apps used by 160 children aged 3-5 found **only 19.6% of apps free of manipulative design**; lures reached 93.1% of children and parasocial pressure 70.6%, with examples including a tearful character in the educational maths app Todo Math and daily-reward lures ([Radesky et al. 2022](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2793493)). PBS KIDS apps had none except autoplay. The paper codes collectibles earned for repeated play as a lure, so Min Bog qualifies in principle; what keeps it benign is no expiry, no daily gating and no purchase path. Companion characters cut both ways: teaching an agent raises effort and makes errors easier to own (the protégé effect, tested in 5th and 8th grade, not preschool) ([Stanford PURL](https://purl.stanford.edu/zm369yx3532)), but a character that begs or grieves is the documented pressure pattern.

## The v1.0 drop-off points at the core loop, not the reward layer

The owner's children stopped playing at v1.0, and production counts about **7 app opens a day** across the whole user base (commits `17e5d5c` and `ddf7b1c`; [usage-analytics.md](../../../docs/usage-analytics.md)). The meaning of that fact is limited — two children, no recorded reason, and per-game route counts sitting unqueried in the production `usage_counter` table — but its direction is informative. v1.0 carried *more* extrinsic machinery than today: stars, personal bests, a round-result screen, round bonus XP and a ring that flashed the next prize. If wrappers held five-year-olds, v1.0 should have held them. The app's own history then removed those layers one by one because "for a five-year-old only [the sticker] means anything" ([Endless Play PRD](../../../plans/endless-play/tmp-prd-endless-play-01-no-round-end.md)). Adding wrappers back would be going in a circle.

Three readings of the drop-off survive, and all point inward. First, sameness: 9 of 16 task games are one tap-a-tile grammar with different content, and the variety of verbs lives almost entirely in Farver and Memory ([current app audit; drag-and-drop.md](../../../.claude/rules/drag-and-drop.md)). Second, fit: the owner's son already knows all letters and counts to 60-70, so recognition quizzes at their lower levels ask him for things he owns — SDT's competence need is fed by optimal challenge, not by easy wins. Third, the weakest games became weak through *deliberate removals*: Tal Quiz's object row, Sammenlign Tal's crocodile and piles, Plus/Minus's ten-frame were each cut because they let the child count blobs instead of reading the numeral ([games-math.md](../../../.claude/rules/games-math.md)). Those were correct educational calls, but they left boards with nothing to do except tap. The way out is not to restore giveaways but to find verbs where the manipulation is the skill. The free first step is cheap and decisive: query the per-route counts already being collected, and watch three to five children play.

## Remake Tal Quiz as Fyld Båden

### Why remake, and why this game

Remaking beats creating because the problem is a flat existing game, not a missing one. A new game would add a 22nd surface and leave the weakest one in place; a remake keeps the menu tile, route, gameId, usage event, XP wiring and practice-ledger history, and changes only the board and the verb. Tal Quiz is the best candidate on every count. It is the thinnest board in the app — a speaker, an equalizer and 3-5 numeral tiles, deliberately showing "NOTHING of the answer" ([games-math.md](../../../.claude/rules/games-math.md)). Its real skill is worth more than its verb: hearing the **inverted Danish number word** ("syvogtredive" = seven-and-thirty), with ranges built around where Danish starts inverting, Let 1-20, Normal 1-50, Svær 1-100 ([difficulty.ts](../../../src/config/difficulty.ts)). Tapping a numeral tests recognition; building the quantity from tens and ones tests whether the child understands what the inverted word *means* — the inversion maps directly onto place value. It is also the skill the owner's son is moving into, since he counts to 60-70. And it is cheap: the 100 bare number clips are already prebaked for Lær Tal.

The runner-up is a **new letter-sound game** ("feed the letter-eater the sound it asks for", after Poio). It probably has the higher learning ceiling for reading, but it is blocked on a risk that can't be settled yet: whether Azure can produce isolated Danish phonemes a child can learn from. Run a listening test on a dozen prebaked phonemes first. If they pass, that game is the next one; if they fail, it is the main reason to buy the voice actor in category B.

### The game in one sentence

A boat glides into the harbour, the narrator says a number, the child loads that many crates onto the deck — whole pallets of ten and single crates — and presses the horn, and the boat sails away with the numeral painted on its sail while the next boat arrives.

### How the learning act is the game action

The child never selects an answer from a list; they **construct** it. "Syvogtredive" requires three pallets and seven crates, so the inversion has to be decoded to load the boat at all, and the classic error — seven pallets and three crates — is the same digit-swap the current distractors test, now made by the child's own hands. The board does not restate its answer, because the number exists only in speech until the boat sails: the crates are the child's construction, not a countable stand-in next to a visible numeral, which is what the owner's rule forbids. The numeral appears on the sail only *after* a correct send, as the reveal-is-the-lesson beat Hvilken Farve? already uses, so the game teaches word → quantity → symbol in that order.

### What the child sees, hears and does

The board is a harbour strip with one boat whose deck is drawn as two rows: a **pallet row of ten slots** and a **ten-frame of single crates** (two rows of five, for subitising). A dock on one side holds an endless pile of pallets and an endless pile of crates. The narrator speaks the bare number using the existing clip; a Hør igen button repeats it. Each crate lands with `drop-snap` and the boat dips slightly (a one-shot animation); the boat bobs at rest through a CSS keyframe (`idleFloat`), never a framer loop. The child taps the boat's horn to send. On a correct send, the narrator says the number again, the numeral is painted on the sail, and the boat sails out. On a wrong send, the boat stays and the narrator speaks the number that is actually loaded ("treoghalvfjerds"), naming the child's construction exactly as Ram Farven names a wrong-but-real mix; then the child adjusts. After two wrong sends the existing never-fail hint applies: the empty slots the target needs glow (`hintPulse`).

### Tap and drag parity

Every action goes through one resolve function, `load(kind)` and `unload(slotId)`. Dragging a pallet or crate from the dock onto the deck calls `load`; **tapping a dock pile** calls the same `load` and fills the next free slot, as Nuancer's tap fills the leftmost empty slot. Dragging a loaded item back to the dock, or tapping it on the deck, calls `unload`. Collision is `kidCollision`, the deck is one large `DroppableZone`, and there is one `DRAG_ACTIVATION_DISTANCE` ([drag-and-drop.md](../../../.claude/rules/drag-and-drop.md)). The horn is a tap-only control, like Hør igen, because it confirms rather than answers. The device check carried over from the tap-drag parity work still applies: a drag must not also register a tap.

### How it plugs into the reward track

Nothing in the reward system changes. A correct send calls `useTaskRun.completeTask`; a wrong send breaks the first-try flag only, so XP stays `taskXp(8, firstTry)` = 5 or 6 per boat, independent of level ([progression.ts](../../../src/config/progression.ts)). If the ring fills, `thenContinue()` holds the advance lock while the boat sails, the shared ceremony plays at that seam, and the next boat arrives after. The header holds the ring alone; nothing counts boats, and the harbour does not accumulate anything — progress stays in Min Bog and in ambient world density. The practice ledger keeps recording missed numbers per item, so a number loaded wrongly comes back a few boats later.

### Content range per level (static, no adaptivity)

| Level | Numbers asked | Dock offers | What is being learned |
|---|---|---|---|
| Let | 1-20 (current Tal Quiz range) | Pallets and crates | Cardinality to 20; "tretten = ti og tre"; first contact with ten as one unit |
| Normal | 1-50 | Pallets and crates | The inverted forms at the scale the child counts to (tyve, tredive, fyrre) |
| Svær | 1-100 | Pallets and crates | The full range including halvtreds, tres, halvfjerds, firs, halvfems — Danish's vigesimal tens |

The ranges are Tal Quiz's existing `MATH_COUNTING.max` values, so no level needs new number narration. The deck capacity is the same at every level (10 pallets, 10 crates), so the board never hints at the target, and the worst case at Svær is 19 actions. `difficulty.test.ts` must sample the generator per level as for every game; a harder level never costs XP.

### Assets: free path and paid upgrade

The free path costs roughly nothing in cash. Art: a boat with a sail that takes a numeral overlay, a crate, a pallet of ten crates (drawn so its ten units are countable), a dock pile and a harbour strip, rendered through the existing Gemini keyed-WebP pipeline in each skin's palette, with numerals left as Typography as on every math board. Sound: horn, splash and crate-thud from Kenney or Sonniss, transcoded to MP3. Narration: the 100 existing bare number clips for prompt, wrong-send feedback and reveal, plus a handful of new fixed lines (an intro such as "Fyld båden med det tal, du hører" and hint lines) through the 8-step prebake protocol ([audio-system.md](../../../.claude/rules/audio-system.md)), approved with `audit:approve-all` and flagged as unheard until the owner listens. The paid path swaps the Gemini boards for a commissioned harbour set (part of the 20,000-50,000 DKK category-B estimate), animates the boat and a captain in Rive (≈ 5,000-17,000 DKK), and adds an actor-voiced captain (≈ 2,000-4,000 DKK plus buyout, and only if the owner relaxes the TTS-only rule).

### Risks

The owner may read the crates as the return of the counting layer he removed four times; the counter-argument is that here the number is never visible and the crates are the answer, not a hint, but the decision is his. The remake drops the numeral-recognition task Tal Quiz tests today, partly recovered by the sail reveal; if the owner wants recognition kept as practice, Sammenlign Tal and Hvad Mangler still read numerals. Svær can turn tedious at 19 actions per boat, which a playtest will show. Pallets that do not *look* like ten will teach nothing, so the art must show ten units clearly. `GameShell` support for a full-bleed board with a moving boat is unverified, and the harbour must be laid out by reserved space, not tuned percentages. Whether five-year-olds return to this game more than to Tal Quiz is a bet, not a finding: compare `route:math_counting` entries before and after, and watch the playtests. Everything here is verified at none of the three device rungs yet; drag feel and the horn's hit area on the iPad are UNKNOWN until the owner tries it.

### Open decisions for the owner

The owner needs to settle six things: the name (*Fyld Båden*, *Lastbåden* or another); whether crates land silently or the narrator counts them as they land (counting along supports one-to-one correspondence at Let but lets the child stop on the sound rather than understand the number); whether pallets are offered at Let or only from Normal; whether the wrong-send feedback speaks the loaded number or only invites a recount; whether to accept the "countable crates" reading of the board rule; and whether the free Gemini path is enough or the commissioned art and Rive captain are worth their cost.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the engagement problem. Børnelæring does not lack rewards; it has already found, by subtraction, the minimal reward layer the evidence favours, and the v1.0 drop-off happened under a richer reward layer than it has now. What it lacks is games in which doing the maths or the letters *is* the fun, and that is a design problem a solo developer can solve almost for free, because the expensive items buy polish, not learning. The strongest single move is to convert flat recognition quizzes into construction verbs that make the child's understanding visible in their hands, starting with Tal Quiz, where Danish's inverted number words give a construction game a real job to do.

Two things should change how the next sessions are judged. A remade game should be measured by whether the child can do something new — load "halvtreds" correctly — rather than by sticker rate, and the per-route counts plus a few watched sessions are enough to tell whether a remake earns its place before the next one starts. And the letter-sound game, the likely biggest literacy win, depends on one cheap listening test of Azure phonemes; it is worth running that test before spending anything in category B.
